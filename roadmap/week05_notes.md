## Shared goal

Find at least one tree that the Rangers can identify as ash, with reasons, and record what a real trunk means for the Tree Stethoscope: its size, bark texture, mounting space, and any visible signs of damage.

## How to recognise an ash tree

- **Branches and buds are opposite**, in pairs across from each other. Few trees have this pattern, so check it first.
- **The leaves are compound**, usually with 5–11 leaflets on one stem. By October, the leaves may be yellow or purple or may already have fallen.
- **Bark on a mature tree** has a criss-cross diamond pattern.
- **Possible EAB signs:** small D-shaped exit holes, light-coloured patches where woodpeckers have pecked, vertical bark splits, thinning at the top of the tree, and new shoots growing from the trunk.

Record possible ash trees as "possible ash" unless a guide or another reliable source confirms the identification. Record possible EAB signs as possible damage, not as a confirmed infestation.

## Park rules and safety

- Follow the park rules and stay on the trails unless permitted otherwise.
- Do not peel bark, cut into a tree, or attach anything that could leave a mark.
- Do not take wood, bark, or firewood from the park. Moving wood can spread EAB.
- Stay with the group and an adult, and agree on a meeting point and time before the walk.
